# Make the storm scene explain itself

The fastest way to an "insanely good" Dial-a-Storm homescreen is to take things away. Build one cinematic scene in which every slider has its own visual channel and the scene teaches the cause-and-effect chain on its own. Put a single hero readout on top ("≈G5 · Extreme, −497 nT, aurora to ~40°"), make one primary button that auto-plays the May 2024 Gannon replay, and merge the chart, scrubber and play controls into one bottom timeline strip. The best references in this space win this way. GitHub's and Stripe's globes use a dark field, a textureless planet, a fresnel halo and one or two accent colors that carry meaning. Igloo Inc won Awwwards Site of the Year 2024 with only three sections. Nobody else fills this niche: NASA's SVS renders are beautiful but you can't interact with them, and NOAA's dashboards are interactive but built for experts. The whole scene fits in about 10–15 draw calls on WebGL2. All motion runs in vertex shaders driven by uniforms. Bloom uses `luminanceThreshold=1`, so only the Sun, the link-up flashes and the aurora glow. A real formula, the Shue magnetopause model, shapes the "shield." On numbers: use the app's own Gannon SYM-H minimum of **−497 nT**. The −412 nT figure in press and Wikipedia is the **hourly Dst index**, a different and smoother measure. Literature SYM-H values run to about −518 nT. Label the hero "SYM-H" and never put a Dst figure next to it.

## Best-in-class references win by subtraction, not features

There are two kinds of official heliophysics visualization, and neither covers what Dial-a-Storm does. NASA's SVS 5435 renders the May 2024 storm from a MAGE/GAMERA simulation, color-coded by meaning: **blue trails for solar-wind velocity, green clouds for electric current, orange-to-purple for field lines**. But it's a video, and the legend lives on the web page, not on the image ([NASA SVS 5435](https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/5435)). SVS 11689 shows a moderate and an extreme CME side by side, which is a direct precedent for a "compare storms" view ([NASA SVS 11689](https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/11689)). SVS 3902 openly says its particles are stylized, because real ions are far too small to see. That shows an honest "illustrative" disclaimer is acceptable practice ([NASA SVS 3902](https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/3902/)). On the interactive side, NOAA's Aurora Dashboard combines OVATION forecasts, solar-wind speed and IMF readings in one page ([SWPC](https://www.spaceweather.gov/news/experimental-aurora-dashboard-available)). NOAA's own scales are being revised because they "have become outdated and confusing" for the public ([Space.com](https://www.space.com/the-universe/earth/space-weather-scales-are-outdated-and-confusing-heres-what-noaa-scientists-are-doing-about-it)). None of these lets someone turn a knob, see the magnetosphere react, and read off one big number. That gap is what Dial-a-Storm can fill.

The transferable craft comes from non-space award winners. GitHub's homepage globe uses **no textures**. It has five layers on a dark base with a two-color accent palette. Its halo is a custom gradient shader on the back face of a sphere at 1.15× scale. Fading dots with camera distance "simulated the look of a thicker atmosphere." It steps down through four quality tiers if it can't hold 55.5 fps, and it paints a static SVG first, then crossfades to WebGL ([GitHub Blog](https://github.blog/engineering/engineering-principles/how-we-built-the-github-globe/)). Stripe's globe grows its arcs over 2.5 s using draw range, turned antialiasing off to fix stutter on 5K displays, and stops rendering when off-screen ([Stripe Blog](https://stripe.com/blog/globe)). Igloo Inc renders its intro with real-time shaders rather than video, compiles shaders in the background, and has **particles that change color with speed and glow as they form shapes** ([Awwwards case study](https://www.awwwards.com/igloo-inc-case-study.html)). That maps directly onto solar-wind particles tinted by speed. earth.nullschool.net shows the minimal version of this formula: a dark globe, moving particles and compact controls ([webiano.digital](https://webiano.digital/earth-nullschool-lets-you-watch-the-planet-breathe/)). The counterweight is Bret Victor's warning that widgets which "dump the user in a sandbox" are not explanations ([Medium summary of Victor](https://medium.com/@Max_Goldstein/exploring-explorable-explanations-92f865c8d6ba)). So the homescreen should open with a guided replay, as JPL's Eyes does with its "journeys," rather than an empty set of sliders ([JPL News](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/explore-the-solar-system-with-nasas-new-and-improved-3d-eyes/)). One limit on all of this: no lay-comprehension study of storm visualizations was found. The evidence is practitioner case studies, award juries and conference posters.

## A four-beat causal story where each slider owns one visual channel

The physics reduces to one accurate chain, told in agency language. The Sun blows a magnetized wind, and Earth's magnetic bubble deflects most of it. When the wind's field points "southward… **opposite the direction of Earth's field**," energy gets in most effectively ([NOAA SWPC](https://www.spaceweather.gov/phenomena/geomagnetic-storms)). ESA describes the mechanism: the fields reconnect on the dayside, the wind drags them tailward, and the drag "stretches the field lines, and stores the energy in the form of magnetic tension." That energy is later released toward Earth ([ESA](https://sci.esa.int/j/36211)). The result is NOAA's "ring of westward current around Earth," which is what the storm indices measure ([NOAA SWPC](https://www.spaceweather.gov/phenomena/geomagnetic-storms)). The single most important lesson to teach before judges touch anything is that **the field's direction matters more than its strength**.

Metaphors need to be chosen on physics grounds, because no tested public-comprehension data exists. "Shield/bubble" works if it is drawn as a translucent, comet-shaped surface that squashes. It should never shatter or leave Earth "exposed." "Opposite magnets snap together" matches NOAA's wording for southward Bz. The "stretched rubber band" comes straight from ESA. The strongest choice is a **bathtub**: the tap is speed × southward field, and the drain is a recovery lasting hours. It matches the Burton equation dDst/dt = Q − Dst/τ, with τ ≈ 7.7 h ([Annales Geophysicae 2008](https://angeo.copernicus.org/articles/26/2543/2008/); [Berkeley](https://sprg.ssl.berkeley.edu/dst_index/estimate.html)). It also explains why the storm number keeps falling after a slider moves. Avoid any picture of solar wind or "radiation" reaching the ground.

Give each input and output its own channel, so effects never blur together:

| Driver / output | Visual channel | Honest anchor |
|---|---|---|
| Southward Bz (the star) | Diverging color plus arrow direction on 5–8 wavy IMF lines (teal north, orange-magenta south). Link-up flashes at the dayside nose only when Bz < 0 | Newell coupling ∝ v^(4/3)·B^(2/3)·sin^(8/3)(θ/2): clock angle gates everything ([ChaosMagPy](https://chaosmagpy.readthedocs.io/en/master/functions/chaosmagpy.coordinate_utils.coupling_Newell.html)). No flashes below V·Bs ≈ 0.5 mV/m (Burton threshold) |
| Speed | Particle velocity and streak length. Color stays neutral so it doesn't compete with Bz | Pdyn ≈ 1.67×10⁻⁶·n·v² nPa |
| Density | Particle draw count (log-scaled) and opacity | Squashes the bubble, but alone rarely makes a storm |
| Shield (magnetopause) | Fresnel shell in the Shue shape, r = r0·(2/(1+cosθ))^α | r0 ≈ **10.3 Re quiet, ~7.6 Re moderate, ~5.7 Re strong** ([Shue 1998 via UCLA](https://faculty.epss.ucla.edu/~ctrussell/russell_bib.html/papers/mpause_extreme/)) |
| Geostationary ring | Dashed ring at 6.6 Re that lights up when the nose crosses inside it ("satellites now outside the shield") | In May 2024 the magnetopause stayed inside GEO for **~6 h** ([Tulasi Ram et al. 2024](https://doaj.org/article/c33b0ecfd1974adb84ec238176663bbc)) |
| SYM-H (ML output) | Ring-current torus whose glow and thickness ease toward the target, not snap | Ring spans ~2–9 Re in the equatorial plane ([ESA](https://sci.esa.int/j/37592)) |
| Aurora (from SYM-H) | Oval colatitude, width and brightness, green moving toward red in big storms | NOAA G2 ~55°, G3 ~50°, G4 ~45°, G5 ~40° geomagnetic ([NOAA scales](https://www.spaceweather.gov/noaa-scales-explanation)). Visual boundary ~51°→44° as the index goes −100→−400 nT ([Zverev](https://seminar.pgia.ru/archive/2009/1_storm%5Cv_l_zverev__.pdf)). Saturates at high activity ([Xiong 2014](https://angeo.copernicus.org/articles/32/609/2014/)) |

Practical defaults for the aurora mapping: oval at ~66° above −30 nT, ~60° at −50, ~52° at −100, ~46° at −250, ~41° at −450, clamped at 40°. This lookup is an inference that combines Kp-based G-scale latitudes with index-based studies. Label any G-tier derived from SYM-H "≈" or "approx." **No source gives a direct Kp↔SYM-H conversion.** The Dst storm classes (moderate −50 to −100 nT, intense −100 to −250, super-storm below −250 nT) come from Gonzalez et al. 1994 and later work ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geomagnetic_storm)). They were defined on Dst but are routinely applied to SYM-H.

The Gannon storm needs one careful note. Sources cite Dst ≈ **−412 nT** at 03:00 UTC on 11 May 2024 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/May_2024_solar_storms)). Papers report SYM-H below −500 nT, around −518 nT, driven by a southward field of about 48 nT ([Tulasi Ram et al. 2024](https://doaj.org/article/c33b0ecfd1974adb84ec238176663bbc)). Other compilations range from −406 to −518 nT ([arXiv 2411.12490](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.12490v2)). Dst is an hourly index, while SYM-H is sampled every minute, so SYM-H minima run deeper, and data versions differ as well. The app's −497 nT SYM-H figure is the one to display, because it is consistent with the model's own target variable. Put "SYM-H" in the details layer or tooltip so a technical judge can see you know the distinction.

Uncertainty should share the prediction's channel rather than add widgets. Spiegelhalter et al. conclude that the best format "depends closely on the objectives… and the audience" ([Science 2011](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011Sci...333.1393S)). Lay users anchor on single numbers ([Millersville poster](https://www.millersville.edu/esci/files/swe20237.pdf)), and aurora chasers say they prefer forecasts with explicit uncertainty language ([CPAESS](https://cpaess.ucar.edu/abstract-2026-sww/initial-survey-results-aurora-chasers-use-space-weather-forecasts-and-uncertainty)). In practice that means four things: a soft band around the predicted line, a feathered equatorward edge on the aurora, one word ("Confidence: High/Medium/Low"), and a hatched "uncharted" zone on any slider pushed past 2010–2026 extremes, with the line "Stronger than anything in our data — the model is guessing." During replays, draw the **observed SYM-H as a ghost line** next to the prediction. Letting judges see the error directly builds more trust than quoting an accuracy figure. One more rule: never let the 3D contradict the number. A G1 prediction with a blazing aurora destroys credibility.

## Ten draw calls and one Bloom deliver the wow on WebGL2

All animation should live in shaders, with uniforms set in `useFrame` from `store.getState()`. The R3F docs warn against `setState` inside `useFrame` and against allocating objects per frame. They also recommend toggling `visible` rather than unmounting, since unmounting forces shader recompiles ([R3F pitfalls](https://r3f.docs.pmnd.rs/advanced/pitfalls)). Each mesh costs a draw call, so use instancing or merged geometry ([R3F scaling](https://r3f.docs.pmnd.rs/advanced/scaling-performance)). Smooth slider changes come from `THREE.MathUtils.damp(u.value, target, 4, dt)` on every uniform, which gives fluid motion with zero React re-renders.

The scene breaks into eight components:

- **Solar wind.** A single stateless `THREE.Points` of 8–20k particles. The vertex shader computes each position as `fract(seed + t·speed)` along the Sun–Earth axis. Any point inside the Shue surface is pushed radially out to it, so the wind visibly slides around the shield. Density is `geometry.setDrawRange(0, n)` and costs nothing. Speed drives `uSpeed` plus streak length. Avoid drei `<Trail>` for many particles, because its history is kept per object on the CPU.
- **Magnetopause.** A unit `SphereGeometry` displaced in the vertex shader to the Shue shape, with the backward angle clamped (`max(cosθ, −0.97)`) so it doesn't run to infinity. The fragment shader is an additive fresnel rim. Compress scale visually (for example r_scene = 2 + 0.35·r0), and add a fainter bow-shock shell at ~1.3× r0. That ratio is a rule of thumb, not a sourced value.
- **Dipole field lines.** Generate them once on the CPU with r = L·cos²λ, then warp them analytically: squash the dayside and stretch the nightside into a tail. Merge everything into one `LineSegments` geometry with an `aT` attribute, and use a single additive shader that sends a bright pulse travelling along each line. Pulse heads above 1.0 are what Bloom catches.
- **Aurora.** A thin shell at r ≈ 1.035 with a Gaussian latitude band around a tilted magnetic pole. Add two simplex-noise octaves for curtain striations and a limb-weighted rim. Optionally stack two outer shells moving from green to red. Raymarched Shadertoy auroras such as nimitz's "Auroras" are a trap: porters report they get expensive at 1080p ([Troikatronix](https://community.troikatronix.com/topic/7442/aurora-borealis-shader)), and Shadertoy's default licence is non-commercial.
- **Ring current.** An additive fresnel `TorusGeometry(3.5, 0.9)` in warm orange, with brightness tied to −SYM-H. Keep it nearly invisible above −30 nT.
- **Earth.** Dark and desaturated: a procedural or 1k land mask, a sun-direction terminator, and a BackSide fresnel atmosphere at fresnel power ~4. The bobbyroe recipe uses bias 0.1, scale 1, power 4 and rim `0x0088ff` ([bobbyroe/threejs-earth](https://github.com/bobbyroe/threejs-earth/blob/main/src/getFresnelMat.js)). A dark Earth makes the aurora and field lines stand out.
- **Sun.** A sphere with three or four fbm octaves at HDR ×3, plus a billboard corona quad. Place it partly off-screen at the left.
- **Stars.** drei `<Stars>` kept dim, below 1, so they don't bloom into noise.

Bloom becomes the only effect, and it is selective. The react-postprocessing docs say that with `luminanceThreshold={1}` nothing glows unless its color exceeds 1 and the material sets `toneMapped={false}` ([react-postprocessing Bloom](https://react-postprocessing.docs.pmnd.rs/effects/bloom)). A good starting point is `<EffectComposer multisampling={0}><Bloom mipmapBlur luminanceThreshold={1} luminanceSmoothing={0.2} intensity={0.8} radius={0.7}/></EffectComposer>`. Push only the Sun core, the flow pulses, the link-up flashes and aurora peaks above 1. Do the vignette as a CSS radial-gradient div rather than a render pass. Fake depth of field by fading particle alpha with depth.

For the camera, drei `<CameraControls>` offers `setLookAt(..., true)` with awaitable promises, which makes a sequenced fly-in easy ([camera-controls README](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/camera-controls@3.1.2/readme.md)). Set up three preset framings (overview, magnetosphere, aurora limb), a ±2–3° idle drift, clamped distances and polar angles, and a disabled wheel-dolly so the user can't get lost. For performance, use `dpr={[1,1.5]}`, `antialias:false`, and drei `PerformanceMonitor` to step DPR and particle count down ([drei docs](https://drei.docs.pmnd.rs/performances/performance-monitor)). Stop rendering when the tab is hidden, and show a poster image when WebGL2 is unavailable. Load the scene through `dynamic(..., {ssr:false})`. Three things are unverified against the installed versions: the normal-pass prop name, which changed across react-postprocessing versions; Line2 `dashOffset` behaviour in three 0.170; and fps on integrated GPUs. Profile at 10k particles before scaling up.

Palette suggestion: background #03050B, Sun #FFB15C→#FFF2D6, wind #FFD9A8, field lines #6FD3FF, aurora #3DFF9A shading to #FF4D8D, ring current #FF8A5C. **Southward Bz is the only magenta/red accent in the UI.**

## One hero number, one button, one timeline strip

NN/g's progressive-disclosure rule settles the layout: "the very fact that something appears on the initial display tells users that it's important." More than two disclosure levels "typically have low usability" ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/)). So the hero sits top-left and is the only large type. It holds a tier chip ("≈G5 · EXTREME"), a big `tabular-nums` number ("−497" with a dimmed "nT"), and one plain-language caption: "Aurora visible down to ~40° — Florida-like latitudes." Everything else (observed vs predicted, peak, V·Bs, the SYM-H/Dst explainer) goes behind one "Show the numbers" disclosure. A "Beyond training data" badge appears attached to the hero only when it applies. Refactoring UI's "labels are a last resort" means the units identify the value and no "SYM-H:" prefix is needed ([Refactoring UI](https://www.refactoringui.com/previews/labels-are-a-last-resort)). The highest-leverage move for comprehension in ten seconds is a set of small **labels anchored in the 3D scene** ("Sun," "Solar wind →," "Magnetic shield," "Storm ring," "Aurora"), so the scene explains itself without a legend. That fixes the off-canvas legend problem SVS has.

For onboarding, NN/g advises avoiding it "whenever possible," found that deck-of-cards tutorials "didn't improve task performance," and favours tips in context ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-app-onboarding/)). So the one filled primary button is "▶ Watch the May 2024 superstorm." It plays the replay with four lower-third captions, each held 3–5 s and each pulsing its matching 3D element: "May 10, 2024: the Sun hurls a giant plasma cloud at Earth" → "Its magnetic field points south — opposite Earth's. That's the dangerous part." → "Earth's shield is squeezed inside satellite orbit. Storm strength −497 nT." → "Auroras reach Florida-like latitudes. Now dial your own storm →", which then pulses the sliders. Keep a visible Skip, and remember "seen" in a localStorage call wrapped in try/catch.

Sliders fit here because "an approximate value is good enough." Their labels go above the track, not below, and presets give an alternative on mobile ([NN/g sliders](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/gui-slider-controls/)). Name them with verbs and plain words: "Magnetic direction (Bz)" with track ends "North = shielded" and "South = storm," "Wind speed (km/s)," "Wind thickness (p/cm³)." Put preset chips above them (Calm day, Gannon 2024, and other historic storms in the 2010–2026 window). Update the 3D on a ~150 ms debounce during the drag so the visual reacts before the model returns.

The chart, scrubber and play controls become one bottom strip, 64–96 px tall. Observed is a white line and the what-if prediction an accent line, both labelled directly at the line ends. The playhead doubles as the scrubber, and faint dashed bands mark the severity tiers. NN/g argues against gauges, which are "harder to interpret than linear graphs," and against encoding magnitude with color alone ([NN/g dashboards](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/dashboards-preattentive/)). Use a five-segment bar with the active tier lit, and always pair the color with text. NOAA defines no G-scale colors, so a proposed ramp is amber-200 → amber-400 → orange-500 → red-500 → fuchsia-500 for G1–G5. Run the repo's dataviz validator on it before shipping. For panels, use one style, `bg-slate-950/75 backdrop-blur-xl border-white/10`. NN/g says glass should be used sparingly and that "more background blur is better" ([NN/g glassmorphism](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/glassmorphism/)). Body text must reach 4.5:1 contrast against the worst-case frame, meaning the Sun glare, not an average frame ([W3C 1.4.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html)). Also respect `prefers-reduced-motion`. MDN notes that "scaling or panning large objects" commonly triggers vestibular symptoms ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/@media/prefers-reduced-motion)). With reduced motion on, run particles at ~0.2× speed, drop the camera drift and fly-in, and fade captions in. On phones, keep the scene full-bleed with a bottom sheet whose peek state shows the timeline and the button.

## Conclusion

The central design insight is that, here, the visualization and the explanation are the same object. When southward Bz owns color and flashes, speed owns motion, density owns count, and the ML number alone drives the storm ring and the aurora oval, a judge can read causality straight off the scene with no legend or tutorial. Physics formulas such as Shue's magnetopause and the G-scale latitudes are therefore design tools: they make each slider movement produce a believable, proportionate response, and that is what separates "pretty" from "credible" for technical judges.

The biggest risk is inconsistency, not a lack of polish: a Dst number shown as SYM-H, a G-tier presented as exact, or a dramatic aurora next to a mild prediction. Any one of these undermines the project's real claim, which is that the model predicts something real. Showing the observed SYM-H ghost line with the app's own −497 nT, flagging the "uncharted" range, and keeping a one-line "illustration, not to scale" disclaimer cost minutes and buy the trust that turns the wow factor into a winning entry.
